How photosynthesis works

A short revision note, used as Lector's demo page.

The big idea

Photosynthesis is how plants, algae and some bacteria turn light into chemical energy. They take in carbon dioxide and water, use the energy of sunlight, and make glucose and oxygen. Almost every food chain on Earth starts here.

The overall equation is 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂. In words: six molecules of carbon dioxide and six of water become one molecule of glucose and six of oxygen.

Where it happens

It happens inside chloroplasts, small structures in leaf cells. Each chloroplast holds stacks of membranes called thylakoids, surrounded by a fluid called the stroma. The green pigment chlorophyll sits in the thylakoid membranes and absorbs mostly red and blue light, which is why leaves look green.

Two stages

The light-dependent reactions happen in the thylakoids. Light splits water, releasing oxygen, and the energy is stored in two carrier molecules, ATP and NADPH.

The Calvin cycle happens in the stroma and does not need light directly. It uses ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into sugar, with the help of an enzyme called RuBisCO, probably the most abundant protein on the planet.

What limits it

Three things set the rate: light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature. Whichever one is in shortest supply is the limiting factor; raising the others does nothing until it is fixed. That is why greenhouses add both light and carbon dioxide.

Too much heat slows it down again, because enzymes such as RuBisCO stop working properly above their best temperature, usually around 25 to 35 degrees Celsius for most plants.

Why it matters

Photosynthesis made the oxygen in our atmosphere, it removes carbon dioxide from the air, and it feeds nearly all life. Fossil fuels are ancient photosynthesis: energy captured by plants millions of years ago.
